# Checklist for Onboarding a New Client at Your Agency

> Client onboarding sets the tone for the entire relationship. Do it right from day one and you'll avoid misunderstandings, delays, and premature cancellations.

## The silent chaos hiding in the first days

Landing a new client is a victory. But what happens in the following two weeks can turn that victory into a headache. Scattered emails, missing documents, pending access, incomplete briefs… Most agencies waste valuable time—and credibility—during the onboarding process because they don't have a clear system.

The problem isn't a lack of professionalism. It's a lack of process. When each onboarding is managed improvised, you depend on the memory of each team member. And memory fails, especially when there are multiple active clients at the same time.

A well-defined onboarding checklist eliminates that noise. It's not bureaucracy: it's the fastest way to show the client that they're in good hands from minute one.

## Phase 1: Before the client signs

Onboarding starts before the contract is signed. As soon as there's a verbal agreement, the clock starts ticking.

**Document the agreement with precision.** The contract must capture the exact scope of service, deliverables, timelines, payment terms, and cancellation clauses. Any ambiguity here becomes conflict later.

**Prepare the financial proposal in writing.** Even if you've closed the price over the phone, the client needs to see it formalized. A signed proposal avoids uncomfortable revisions down the road.

**Collect tax and billing information from the start.** Don't wait until month-end to discover you need the tax ID or address. Ask for them along with the contract signature.

Digital signatures accelerate this step enormously. With a tool that lets you send, sign, and store contracts from one place, the process goes from taking days to taking minutes.

## Phase 2: The first days after onboarding

Once the contract is signed, the real integration work begins.

**Kickoff meeting with structured agenda.** The first meeting can't be an open-ended conversation without direction. Prepare an agenda that covers: client objectives, success metrics, preferred communication channels, reporting frequency, and contact people on both sides.

**Complete brief in writing.** Everything the client shares in that meeting must be documented. If it's not written down, it doesn't exist. Send them a brief summary and ask for explicit confirmation that it reflects what was agreed.

**Create the client file.** Open a folder or centralized space where all documents go: contract, proposal, brief, credentials, brand assets. The entire team should know where this is without having to ask.

**Assign a clear internal owner.** The client needs to know who to contact. Internally, someone must own that account. Shared responsibilities without assignment are responsibilities no one assumes.

**Set up initial project tasks.** Convert the contract deliverables into concrete tasks with dates and owners. If this is left in the air, deadlines get missed without anyone noticing until too late.

## Phase 3: The startup week

**Share access and tools.** If the client will review progress, they need access to the correct spaces with the correct permissions. Neither more nor less. Define this from the beginning to avoid urgent access requests at the worst time.

**Establish the official communication channel.** Email? A management platform? Whatever it is, but let it be just one. Clients who manage projects through WhatsApp, email, and calls at the same time create chaos. Propose and lead the system yourself.

**Send a first status update.** Even if there's nothing relevant to report, communicate that the project has started, that the team is aware, and that the next update will be on such a date. This reduces client anxiety and "how's it going?" emails.

**Schedule the first check-in.** Put the follow-up meeting in the calendar two or four weeks out now. Don't leave it for when the client requests it.

## Conclusion

A good onboarding process isn't a luxury for large agencies. It's what separates agencies that retain clients from those that are always looking for new ones.
