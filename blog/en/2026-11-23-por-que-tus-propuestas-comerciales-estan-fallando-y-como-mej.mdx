# Why Your Business Proposals Are Failing and How to Improve Them

> You send proposals, the client says "we'll review it" and disappears. The problem is almost never the price: it's in how you present the value.

## The silence after sending the proposal

You know this situation well. You have a meeting that goes well, the client shows interest, you ask for their details to send them the proposal and… silence. A follow-up email, then another, maybe a call. Nothing.

The usual reaction is to think the price was too high or that the competition offered something better. Sometimes that's true. But very often the problem comes earlier: in the proposal itself. In what you say, how you say it, and when you send it.

A business proposal is not a budget with letterhead. It's the moment when the client decides whether they trust you enough to give you their money. If that document doesn't work for you, you're wasting opportunities you already had half won.

## The most common mistakes that ruin a proposal

**Talking about yourself instead of talking about the client.** The most widespread mistake. The proposal starts with the company's history, years of experience, awards received. The client didn't open that document to learn who you are: they opened it to see if you can solve their problem. Always start with a diagnosis of their situation, not your resume.

**Not making clear what problem you solve.** A proposal full of services and deliverables without connecting them to a concrete result is a shopping list, not a solution. The client needs to understand what changes in their business if they hire you. If they don't read it in the first few paragraphs, they stop reading.

**Price without context.** Presenting a figure without having previously justified the value behind it shoots up resistance. The price should appear when the client has already understood what they gain, not as the first impact.

**Generic proposals.** If you copy and paste the same structure for all clients changing only the name and the amount, the client notices. A personalized proposal doesn't mean writing it from scratch every time, but it does mean the client's specific problem should be the axis of the entire document.

**Sending it too late.** The client's interest has a window. If you take a week to send the proposal after the meeting, that interest has cooled, they've spoken with other suppliers, and you've lost momentum.

## What makes a proposal that converts different

A good business proposal follows a simple logic: problem → solution → result → price → next step.

**Open with the diagnosis.** Show that you've understood where the client is now and what it's costing them to be there. Two or three sentences that make them think "exactly, that's what's happening to us" generate more trust than three pages of corporate presentation.

**Make the expected results concrete.** Don't sell hours or deliverables: sell the before and after. "Reducing contract management time from three days to less than one" is infinitely more powerful than "contract review and drafting."

**Structure the price in options.** Presenting two or three levels of proposal shifts the conversation from "do I hire or not?" to "which level fits better?" It's not always possible, but when it is, it works.

**Include a clear next step.** The proposal has to end with a concrete action: a call to resolve questions, a proposed start date, a pending signature. Without a defined next step, the client doesn't know what to do and does nothing.

**Care about formatting.** A clean, well-structured, and easy-to-read document conveys professionalism before the client reads a single line of content. You don't need sophisticated design, but you do need visual consistency and no indigestible blocks of text.

## Habits that make a difference day to day

- **Create base templates** by client type or service. You save time and maintain consistency without sacrificing personalization.
- **Send the proposal within 24-48 hours** following the meeting, when interest is still hot.
- **Schedule a follow-up** at the moment you send it. Don't leave that reminder to memory.
- **Ask for feedback when a client doesn't accept.** Not to insist, but to learn. Many will give it to you if you ask honestly.
