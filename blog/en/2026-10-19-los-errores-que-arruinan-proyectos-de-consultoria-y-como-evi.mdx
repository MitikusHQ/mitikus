# The mistakes that ruin consulting projects (and how to avoid them)

> Managing a consulting project seems simple until it isn't. These are the most frequent failures that cost time, money, and clients.

## When chaos arrives in disguise as a project

A consulting project starts with energy: kick-off meeting, approved proposal, enthusiastic client. Three weeks later, nobody knows exactly what's pending, the client asks via email things that were already answered in a meeting, and the team works with different versions of the same document.

This scenario is not exceptional. It is, in fact, the norm in many offices and consulting firms that bill well but manage poorly. The problem is usually not the team's technical capacity, but the processes—or lack thereof—that surround the work.

Identifying specific mistakes is the first step to not repeating them. Here are the most common ones.

## Errors in the initiation and definition phase

**Scope poorly defined from the start**
The most expensive mistake of all. When the project scope is not documented with precision—what it includes, what it doesn't, how many reviews are contemplated—the client will always ask for more and the team will always give in. The result is unbilled work and exhausted teams.

The solution is simple though uncomfortable: a signed scope document before starting. Not an email, a formal document with a signature. This protects both parties and eliminates ambiguities from day one.

**Client objectives not translated into concrete deliverables**
"I want to improve my sales process" is not a project objective. It's a wish. Consulting begins when that wish is translated into measurable deliverables: a process diagnosis in week 3, a procedures manual in week 6, a training session in week 8.

Without this translation, the project moves forward without success criteria. And when the client is not satisfied, nobody knows exactly why.

## Errors in execution and monitoring

**Reactive communication instead of proactive**
Many consultants only contact the client when they have something to deliver or when there's a problem. This generates uncertainty in the client, who starts to ask questions, to doubt, and eventually to lose confidence.

A simple weekly progress report—even if it's just five lines—completely changes the client's perception. They don't seek constant perfection; they seek transparency and control.

**Task management via email**
Email is a communication channel, not a project management tool. When tasks, deadlines, and responsibilities live in email threads, things inevitably get lost. And when something gets lost in consulting, it usually costs money or reputation.

Tasks need their own system: assigned to one person, with a visible deadline and updated status. Without that system, the team works with incomplete information.

**Not tracking the time spent on each project**
Without real hour tracking, it's impossible to know if a project is profitable or not. Many consulting firms discover, when closing the year, that their most "successful" projects in terms of client satisfaction were the least profitable in real terms. Time tracking by project is not bureaucracy: it's business intelligence.

## Errors in closure and documentation

**Closing projects without a retrospective**
When a project ends, the team moves to the next one without analyzing what worked and what didn't. The opportunity to learn is lost, to improve the estimation of future projects and to detect what types of clients or assignments are not profitable.

A 30-minute retrospective when closing each project, with three basic questions—what went well, what failed, and what we would do differently—is worth more than any external training.

**Scattered or unsigned contracts and documents**
The contract signed at the start, the scope annexes, the delivered reports... in many offices these documents live in desktop folders, inboxes, and in the worst case, only in the memory of whoever managed the project. When a conflict or audit arises, document disorganization becomes a real problem.

Centralizing contracts, proposals, and deliverables in a single system—with clear versions and digital signatures when appropriate—is not an exaggeration. It's the bare minimum required to work professionally.
